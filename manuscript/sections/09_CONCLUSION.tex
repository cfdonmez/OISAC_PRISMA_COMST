\section{CONCLUSION}
\label{sec:conclusion}

O-ISAC design begins with the physical interaction that makes sensing possible
and the resource that connects it to communication. A fiber can carry traffic
and respond to a disturbance; an optical beam can transmit data and probe a
scene; photonic hardware can create a shared reference for a wireless link.
These roles lead to different constraints, even when the systems use similar
waveforms or report similarly named metrics. Across the reviewed evidence,
joint performance follows from how a design
choice changes both the useful observation and its competing demands on
power, bandwidth, timing, and receiver capability. Resource allocation can
create interference or reduce payload capacity, while sensing information can
also support transmission and control. The resulting benefit depends on the
task, measurement plane, resource budget, and operating conditions. These
mechanisms provide a basis for selecting and interpreting designs without
implying a universal performance frontier across platforms.

The next step is to test whether these relations persist under the traffic,
motion, propagation, and hardware conditions of the intended application.
Paired measurements, suitable separate-function baselines, and reconstructable
experiments can expose where a joint advantage holds, where it fails, and why.
Extending those tests through acquisition, processing, and network control
would connect optical integration to demonstrated service value.
