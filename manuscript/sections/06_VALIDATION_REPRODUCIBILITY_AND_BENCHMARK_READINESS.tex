\section{JOINT VALIDATION UNDER REALISTIC CONDITIONS}
\label{sec:validation_reproducibility}

The performance relations in Section~\ref{sec:tradeoffs} identify how a shared
resource or design choice changes communication and sensing. Their usefulness
for another system depends on whether the relation survives the disturbances,
hardware effects, and service demands of that system. Validation must therefore
connect the controlled design choice to both measured outcomes and explain how
the operating conditions were exercised. The central question is how much of
that joint behavior has been demonstrated, from a numerical model to a deployed
link, and how another group could test it again.

\subsection{What Different Validation Settings Reveal}

Figure~\ref{fig:validation_profile} summarizes each study's highest reported
setting. Laboratory and proof-of-concept work accounts for 78 studies, and
controlled prototypes for 66. Simulation or numerical work accounts for 32,
enhanced simulation or dataset-based work for 18, and field trials or deployments
for 12. These exclusive settings describe the reach of the reported evaluation;
a single study can also combine analysis, simulation, and physical experiments.
The complete overlapping method profile is retained in the electronic supplement.

Each setting exposes a different part of the coupling mechanism. Simulation
can isolate waveform allocation and device sensitivities while holding other
parameters fixed \cite{OISAC_SCR00007,OISAC_SCR00274}. Laboratory and prototype
implementations expose conversion, synchronization, and receiver effects that
enter the physical signal chain
\cite{OISAC_SCR00001,OISAC_SCR00210,OISAC_SCR00362}. Field studies add the
constraints of access networks, deployed fiber, submarine monitoring, and
outdoor wireless links
\cite{OISAC_SCR00496,OISAC_SCR00631,OISAC_SCR00957,OISAC_SCR01054,OISAC_SCR01086}.
The scientific contribution of a field trial depends on which of these
conditions it actually tests. A controlled prototype may sweep a larger range
of a shared parameter or measure the two functions more directly than a field
dataset collected for a single outcome.

The required disturbances follow the physical path. In fiber, topology,
traffic, launch conditions, receiver operation, and the disturbance being
sensed define the experiment. For VLC and LiFi, geometry, receiver orientation,
illumination, and detector response determine the available observation. FSO
adds pointing, target properties, atmosphere, and motion. Photonics-enabled
terahertz systems cross an additional interface: optical conversion and
reference stability must be related to antenna alignment and wireless
propagation. Following the same configuration through analysis, simulation,
and experiment helps distinguish a predicted resource interaction from an
unmodeled component or environmental effect.

\subsection{Field Coverage of Both Functions}

Among the 12 field or deployment studies, six report outcomes in both
functional domains at that level. They cover photonics-enabled terahertz and
cellular links, fiber access integrated with radio-frequency identification,
space laser ranging and communication, deployed multicore fiber, and submarine
cable monitoring
\cite{OISAC_SCR01054,OISAC_SCR00496,OISAC_SCR00631,OISAC_SCR00730,OISAC_SCR01086,OISAC_SCR00957}.
Thus, field evidence for communication and sensing extends across several
physical settings. This finding establishes coverage at the study level; it
does not make all six systems equivalent joint demonstrations.

Concurrent joint operation requires the two outcomes to refer to the same
configuration, time interval, and condition set. If a communication result
comes from one test and a sensing result from another, their coexistence in a
paper supports a different claim. Electronic Supplement S7 preserves the
separate source locators and available timing information for the two
functions. This distinction also matters when the environment is physical but
the downstream processing is performed later. One fiber study combines field
data with offline sensing classification \cite{OISAC_SCR01006}. The data show
what was observed on the link, while the classification evaluates a subsequent
processing stage; collection and functional execution occupy different parts
of the evaluation.

For a segmented system, the same reasoning extends across synchronization,
conversion, and calibration interfaces. A joint test should show how each
interface maps the transmitted signal and physical disturbance to the two
reported outcomes. This makes it possible to identify whether a degradation
originates in the shared optical resource, the wireless path, or subsequent
processing, and to judge whether service continues during the disturbance.

Another group needs the waveform and processing chain, hardware configuration,
scene and calibration, metric definitions, and data used to obtain that value.

Open data appear in simulation, laboratory, prototype, computer-vision, and
field-data settings
\cite{OISAC_SCR00274,OISAC_SCR00362,OISAC_SCR00833,OISAC_SCR01006,OISAC_SCR01008}.
They permit reanalysis of the released observations. Repeating the physical
experiment additionally requires the acquisition and calibration conditions,
and reproducing a processed result requires the transformation from inputs to
outputs. The computer-vision study reporting partial code or model components
illustrates this distinction between an available component and a complete
execution path \cite{OISAC_SCR00833}.

We used the Technical Quality Assessment Framework (TQAF) developed for this
review to describe such support. It scores eight technical and reporting
dimensions from 0 to 3 and records overall evidence contribution separately.
TQAF is a review-specific descriptive appraisal whose scoring has not been
independently validated; it is not a conventional risk-of-bias assessment.
The full profile and scoring rules are provided in the electronic supplement.
Its reproducibility dimension combines parameter reporting with artifact
access: 13 studies report open data, whereas three meet its strong
reproducibility criteria. The difference reflects the additional information
needed to reconstruct the system and its analysis.

\subsection{From Reconstruction to a Shared Benchmark}

A reconstructable experiment becomes a common test when independent designs
can address the same task under a stated baseline, calibration, and metric
definition. This requires agreement on which variables are controlled and
which are allowed to change. For example, repeating a power-allocation result
requires the same interpretation of the power budget; repeating a tracking
result also requires the geometry and update process that produced the
observation. The comparison conditions established in
Section~\ref{sec:foundations_comparison} are therefore part of the experiment.

No study reached the strong benchmark-readiness category under TQAF's combined
criteria for common baselines, open artifacts, admissible outcomes in both
domains, validation, and reproducibility. This review-specific result identifies
an opportunity to combine capabilities already demonstrated separately. Shared
tasks and recoverable experiments would allow the application requirements in
Section~\ref{sec:technologies_applications_6g} to be tested against the same
physical performance relation, rather than inferred from unrelated peak values.
