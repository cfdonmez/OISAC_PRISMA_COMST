## Findings

### P01

Section II, “Operating Spectrum Across RF and Optical,” begins immediately with II-A “RF-Based ISAC.” There is **no separate Section II lead paragraph**. Its first actual prose paragraph begins with the RF operating range and walks through transmitter generation/modulation, RF chain, radiation, receiver, digitization and DSP (lines 234–249, PDF p.3). This is an entry through a concrete physical chain. It does not repeat the broad importance of ISAC from the introduction.

Its connection to the paper is made BEFORE Section II. The introduction's contribution is the evolution from RF to optical (185–188), the overall research gap is the missing multidimensional evolutionary view (169–182), and the roadmap announces the RF/optical comparison (225–233). Thus II can start directly with technology.

The chapter follows RF (including antenna architectures and RIS) → optical → hybrid RF–optical → lessons. More usefully, each major technological family contains “Summary and Discussion.” The transition is causal: RF limitations motivate optical exploration (366–386, PDF p.5); optical environmental/alignment and evaluation constraints motivate cross-domain design (450–481, pp.6–7); hybrids raise coordination and physical integration requirements (565–596, p.8). “Lessons Learned” turns those observations into three research questions (600–625, p.8), including how performance measures and optimization objectives relate.

This Section II is an early **review and synthesis chapter**, not a short preparatory foundations chapter. Its length is therefore not a direct target for our Section II. The useful structural lesson is clear subject → physical explanation → limitation → next design need → synthesis. It is unnecessary to copy a large RF overview into our O-ISAC survey.

### P02

Section 2, “Categories of O-ISAC,” has a two-sentence lead that states the classification axes, names VLC/FSO/photonic sensing and points to the figure (388–392, PDF p.3). It moves straight into descriptions and study examples for those categories (393–458), ending by restating the comparison axes and referring to Table 4 (614–616, p.6). This is a taxonomy-and-examples opening, closer to our modality chapter than to our foundations chapter.

Our foundations material is distributed over P02 Sections 4 and 5:

- Section 4 lead: integration architecture and promised benefits → architecture and waveform discussion (1005–1009, p.11). Architecture paragraph follows generation, propagation, reflection, detection and recovery (1011–1029); waveform discussion explains the real/nonnegative IM/DD restriction before waveform families (1166–1174, p.12).
- Section 5 lead: short catalogue of hardware, metrics, hybrid systems, modulation and learning (1224–1228, p.13). Metrics discuss different purposes of communication and sensing and move toward a proposed unified measure (1230–1238 and 1255–1293, pp.13–14).

Its connection to motivation is primarily coverage: the introduction promises categories, architecture, fundamentals and applications (366–384), then the corresponding sections deliver them. This differs from our stronger potential connection: physical diversity makes design choices and reported outcomes difficult to interpret across optical systems.

P02 is a useful subject-near example but should not be copied mechanically. Its broad “generalized” architecture in Section 4 is specifically an IM/DD free-space/reflection chain. It does not cover all the signal roles in our fiber, optical wireless and photonics-enabled wireless scope. Our separation between actual propagation and optical waveform generation, and between direct/coherent observation, should be retained. P02 also advocates a unified metric; our survey can develop common questions and measurement definitions without claiming that fiber vibration and free-space ranging share one score.

## Implication for our Section II

Our current four-step sequence is useful and coherent: observations → sharing/optical constraints → measurement meanings → joint operating points. It is shorter than P01's review chapter and should not be expanded solely to match it. Neither comparator establishes a COMST standard lead length: one has none and the other a very short lead in another journal.

The main opening issue is continuity, not word count. The current first sentence repeats the O-ISAC definition already given in I-A. The next sentence names three determinants, and most of the rest describes what this section will do. Meanwhile I-A's final paragraph already asks a stronger question: which design choice produced the reported result, how did both functions respond, and under what conditions? (Introduction lines 51–60.) Section II can answer that question immediately.

For discussion before editing: start from the technical reason the foundations are needed. A shared resource has different physical roles; the signal path and receiver determine the observable, optical constraints determine feasible sharing, and those choices determine what paired performance means. Then state the section's route briefly. Retain the first concrete fiber/visible-light/photonics examples in II-A rather than duplicating them all in the opening. One paragraph around 80–110 words is a plausible editorial choice, not a source-derived requirement.

The final paragraph (current lines 323–337) already links comparison to compatible tasks/measurements and to following chapters. It could close with one technical takeaway before the roadmap, so the reader remembers why propagation, receiver observation and shared settings form the basis for the later architecture and tradeoff analysis. Avoid replacing this physical storyline with eligibility or audit terminology.
